## Answer

The earliest evidence of systematic visual communication that approaches writing appears in the administrative practices of southern Mesopotamia during the late fourth millennium BCE. Clay tokens of various shapes had long been used to represent quantities of grain, livestock, and other goods. Around 3400 BCE these tokens were sealed inside clay envelopes, and the contents were impressed on the exterior so that the record could be checked without breaking the seal. Soon the impressions themselves became the primary record, and the tokens were abandoned.

These early markings were largely pictographic, each sign standing for a concrete object or a countable unit. Over the next few centuries the repertoire expanded and the forms were simplified. Scribes began pressing a reed stylus into the soft clay at an angle, producing the characteristic wedge-shaped strokes that give the script its later name, cuneiform. By roughly 2900 BCE the signs no longer functioned only as pictures of things; many had acquired phonetic values, allowing scribes to write the sounds of the Sumerian language as well as pure ideas.

This dual logographic-syllabic character made it possible to record not merely inventories but also personal names, legal formulas, and eventually continuous narrative. The same system was later adapted to Akkadian and several other languages of the Near East, demonstrating that a writing technology developed for one tongue could be transferred to others. Administrative archives from Uruk, Jemdet Nasr, and later cities show that the new tool was used first and foremost for economic control—tracking deliveries, labor obligations, and temple estates—rather than for literature or private correspondence.

The social consequences were immediate. Once records could be consulted and compared across seasons, larger-scale planning became feasible. Specialized training was required to master the hundreds of signs, so scribal schools emerged and writing became a professional skill restricted to a small elite. The clay tablet, durable yet easily produced, created an archive that outlasted any single official. In this way the first true writing system both reflected and reinforced the hierarchical organization of the early city-state, converting fleeting oral agreements into permanent, inspectable documents.
