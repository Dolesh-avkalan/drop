## Answer

Chinese writing developed independently in the Yellow River valley and is first attested in the oracle-bone inscriptions of the late Shang dynasty, around 1200 BCE. These early texts, carved on turtle plastrons and ox scapulae for divinatory purposes, already display a mature logographic system in which each character corresponds to a word or morpheme rather than to a single sound. Many signs are recognizably pictorial in origin, yet even at this stage a substantial number function as phonetic loans or as compounds that combine a semantic element with a sound indicator.

Over the following centuries the script expanded to meet the needs of a growing bureaucracy and a lengthening literary tradition. By the Western Zhou period bronze inscriptions record royal grants and ritual formulas in a more regularized hand. The Qin unification in 221 BCE imposed a standard small-seal script that reduced regional variation, and the subsequent Han dynasty refined the clerical script whose brush-written forms underlie the modern regular script. Throughout these changes the fundamental unit remained the character: a discrete graph that carries both meaning and, in most cases, a pronunciation. Because Chinese morphology is largely isolating, the logographic principle proved stable; characters could be read across divergent spoken dialects and even across centuries of sound change.

The social organization of literacy reflected the script’s demands. Mastery of several thousand characters required prolonged study, so education remained the pathway to official appointment under the imperial examination system. At the same time the characters’ relative independence from any single pronunciation allowed a shared written language to bind together populations whose spoken varieties were mutually unintelligible. Administrative documents, classical commentaries, and later vernacular fiction all circulated in the same graphic medium. When woodblock printing spread in the Tang and Song periods, the existing character set could be reproduced without the need to invent movable type for an alphabet, accelerating the dissemination of texts while preserving the logographic structure that had already endured for more than two millennia.
