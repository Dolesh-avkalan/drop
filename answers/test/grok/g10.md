## Answer

The decisive simplification that produced the alphabet took place along the eastern Mediterranean during the second millennium BCE. Earlier writing systems required hundreds of signs because each character could stand for a whole word or a syllable. Around 1800 BCE, Semitic-speaking workers and traders in the Sinai and Canaan began to reduce the Egyptian hieroglyphic repertoire to a much smaller set of signs that represented individual consonants. The resulting Proto-Sinaitic script, attested in brief inscriptions at Serabit el-Khadim and elsewhere, already shows the core principle of an abjad: each letter stands for a single sound, and the reader supplies the vowels from context.

By the late second millennium this experiment had matured into the Phoenician alphabet of twenty-two letters. Phoenician merchants carried the script across the Mediterranean, where it was adapted first by the Greeks and later by other peoples. The Greek innovation was to repurpose certain unused consonant signs as vowels, creating the first true alphabet in the modern sense. From the Greek version descended the Etruscan and Latin scripts, while Aramaic variants spread eastward and ultimately supplied the ancestors of the Hebrew, Arabic, and various Indic alphabets. In each case the underlying economy remained the same: a few dozen signs could encode an entire language once the principle of one sign per phoneme was established.

The social consequences were far-reaching. Because the alphabet could be learned in weeks rather than years, literacy ceased to be the exclusive preserve of palace or temple scribes. Merchants kept their own accounts, soldiers scratched messages on ostraca, and eventually private individuals composed letters and poetry. Administrative empires still employed professional clerks, yet the barrier to entry had fallen dramatically. At the same time the alphabet’s simplicity encouraged the spread of written law, religious texts, and commercial contracts beyond the old elite circles. The same economy that made the script easy to master also made it easy to transmit across linguistic boundaries, which is why a single family of alphabets came to dominate so much of Eurasia and North Africa while older logographic systems persisted mainly in East Asia.
