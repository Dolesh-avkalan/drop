Bridges are built to move. Not because they are unstable, but because the materials they are made from respond to heat, cold, traffic and time. Steel and concrete expand when warm and contract when cool. A steel girder spanning a few hundred metres can change length by several centimetres between a winter night and a summer afternoon. If that span were locked rigidly at both ends, the movement would have nowhere to go. The resulting forces would be enormous — enough to crack concrete, buckle beams, shear bolts, or lift a bearing off its seat.

Expansion joints are the deliberate gaps that let a bridge accommodate this movement safely. They sit at the ends of spans, between sections, and at the approaches, allowing the deck to lengthen and shorten without fighting its own supports. They also absorb other movements: the slow sag under load, small rotations as traffic crosses, and the subtle shifts caused by settling foundations or seismic activity.

Beyond temperature, joints manage vibration and wear. As vehicles roll on and off the deck, the structure flexes; joints give that motion somewhere to go rather than concentrating stress at a single point. They also keep water and debris from reaching vulnerable bearings and piers below, which is why modern joints are fitted with seals and drainage.

The cost of ignoring this is real. Rigid bridges develop cracks, misaligned decks and premature failure. Expansion joints are therefore not a weakness in a bridge's design — they are what allow a stiff structure to survive decades of constant, invisible movement.
