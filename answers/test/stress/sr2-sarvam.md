Bridges are built to move. Not because they are unstable, but because the materials they are made of — steel, concrete and asphalt — expand when heated and contract when cooled. Over a span of several hundred metres, that movement is substantial. Steel expands by roughly 12 parts per million for every degree Celsius of temperature change. On a 500-metre bridge, a 40°C swing between a cold winter night and a hot summer afternoon can translate into about 240 mm of length change, nearly a quarter of a metre. Without somewhere for that movement to go, enormous forces would build up inside the structure, cracking the deck, distorting the bearings and, in extreme cases, buckling the roadway.

Expansion joints are the deliberate gaps that absorb this motion. They are installed at intervals along the deck and at the abutments where the bridge meets the approach roads. A joint may be a simple asphalt-filled gap on a small bridge, a finger joint with interlocking steel teeth on a long span, or a complex modular unit with several sliding seals on a cable-stayed structure. Their job is to let the deck lengthen and shorten while keeping the riding surface smooth and waterproof.

They also accommodate other movements. Traffic loading deflects the deck downward, wind pushes it sideways, and earthquakes shake it in every direction. Prestressed concrete creeps and shrinks over the years. Expansion joints, together with bearings, absorb all of this so that the main structure is never forced to resist motion it cannot tolerate.
