## Answer

Bridges face constant exposure to changing weather and loads that make their materials grow and shrink. Expansion joints are built into the structure specifically to absorb these shifts so the bridge does not crack or buckle under its own stress. Temperature changes are the main driver. On hot days concrete and steel lengthen; on cold days they shorten. A long bridge can move several inches between extremes. Without joints that movement builds enormous internal forces that eventually split the deck, warp girders, or damage the supports.

Joints also handle other kinds of motion. Heavy trucks, wind, and earthquakes cause the structure to flex, rock, or settle slightly at different points. The joints let those small displacements happen locally instead of transmitting damaging forces all the way to the fixed ends. Over years concrete also creeps and shrinks, and the joints give the material room to adjust without tearing itself apart.

Well-designed joints keep the bridge usable and durable. They must stay strong enough for traffic yet flexible enough to move, and they need regular cleaning so debris does not jam them. When they work correctly they protect the rest of the investment, keep water out of the reinforcing steel, and let the bridge stay in service for decades with far fewer major repairs.
