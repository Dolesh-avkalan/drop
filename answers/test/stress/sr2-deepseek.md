## Answer

Bridges are exposed to constant changes in temperature, and their materials—most commonly steel and concrete—expand when heated and contract when cooled. Without expansion joints, these movements would be trapped within the structure, creating enormous internal forces. Over time, that stress can crack concrete, buckle steel, damage bearings, and even compromise the bridge’s overall stability. Expansion joints give the bridge a controlled way to lengthen and shorten without fighting itself.

Beyond temperature, bridges also move under traffic loads, wind, earthquakes, and even subtle shifts in their foundations. Expansion joints allow small movements at specific points, usually between sections of the deck or between the deck and the abutments. This keeps the roadway smooth and prevents one part of the structure from pushing or pulling against another. They also help protect the approach slabs and supporting piers from unnecessary wear.

Modern expansion joints must do more than just open and close. They need to keep water and debris out, reduce noise, and support heavy vehicles without creating a bump. If a joint fails, the bridge may still stand, but it can deteriorate faster and become uncomfortable or unsafe for drivers. Regular inspection and maintenance of these joints are therefore essential. In short, expansion joints are not a weakness—they are what allow a rigid-looking bridge to survive a lifetime of movement.
