For as long as people have sailed, coastlines have been dangerous. Rocky shores, hidden reefs, and fogbound headlands wrecked ships and drowned sailors who had no way of knowing how close they were to land. Lighthouses were built to answer that problem: to mark a fixed point on a shifting, featureless sea and warn mariners of hazards before it was too late.

Their earliest purpose was simple navigation. A tower with a fire at its top, visible for miles, told a captain where the shore began and where a safe channel ran. As trade grew, so did the stakes. Ports depended on ships arriving reliably, and merchants, navies, and fishing fleets all needed guidance through narrow straits and river mouths. A light could turn a fatal stretch of coast into a passable route, and a harbour into a busy one.

Lighthouses also carried a human duty. Wrecked crews, lost in storms, had little chance of rescue without a beacon to steer toward. A steady light meant shelter, food, and dry land. Builders therefore placed them where danger was greatest and kept them burning through the worst weather, often at great cost and risk to the keepers themselves.

In an age before satellites and radar, a lighthouse was the most reliable warning a sailor could get. It was built for safety, for commerce, and for the simple promise that someone on shore was watching. Even now, though GPS has replaced much of their work, lighthouses remain enduring symbols of that vigilance.
