Bridges are built the way they are because every span must solve one problem: carrying a load across an obstacle while resisting the forces that try to pull it apart. The design that emerges is a negotiation between gravity, geometry, and the materials at hand.

A bridge's shape follows the way it handles stress. In a beam bridge, the deck bends under load, so the top is squeezed in compression while the bottom is stretched in tension. A truss refines this by arranging triangles that carry the same forces more efficiently. An arch bridge redirects the downward weight into compression along its curve, pushing outward into massive abutments that hold the thrust. A suspension or cable-stayed bridge does the opposite: steel cables carry the deck in tension and hang it from towers, letting a single span leap a wide gap. Each material has limits — concrete resists compression well, steel resists tension — and the best designs place each where it works hardest.

The site decides much of the rest. Builders weigh the width and depth of the crossing, the strength of the ground, the water's current, wind, and earthquake risk. Cost, maintenance, and traffic demand narrow the options further. A short urban crossing favours a simple, cheap beam; a vast strait demands towers and cables.

So a bridge is never arbitrary. It is the visible answer to a precise set of forces and conditions — an engineering compromise that turns physics into something you can walk across.
