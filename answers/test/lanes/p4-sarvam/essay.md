Before movable type, knowledge travelled at the speed of a scribe's hand. Books were copied one at a time, often by monks, and a single volume could take months to produce. This made texts scarce, expensive, and fragile. Libraries were treasures guarded by the wealthy and the clergy, and reading was a privilege few could claim.

Johannes Gutenberg's press, developed around 1440, changed this fundamentally. By casting individual metal letters that could be rearranged and reused, printing turned a slow craft into a fast, mechanical process. Suddenly hundreds of identical copies could be produced in the time it once took to make one. The cost of a book fell sharply, and literacy spread far beyond the elite.

The consequences were profound. Ideas could now be reproduced exactly, so errors no longer multiplied with each hand-copy. Scholars across Europe could read the same text, compare notes, and build on one another's work. The Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the Enlightenment all drew energy from the printed page. Pamphlets and newspapers carried argument and news to ordinary people, feeding public opinion and political change. Printed in vernacular tongues, texts reached people in the languages they actually spoke.

Printing did not merely store knowledge; it standardised and multiplied it. It created a shared intellectual world in which ideas could be tested, disputed, and preserved. In doing so, it reshaped who could learn, what could be known, and how quickly understanding advanced — a transformation whose echoes still shape the information age we live in today.
